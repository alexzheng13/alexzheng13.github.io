What matters when time is running out: regrets, companionship, or simply more time? What becomes of hope when recovery is no longer possible?

This account follows hospice physician Christopher Kerr’s Diary Of A CEO interview in its original sequence.

Interview published October 19, 2026. Cover from the original video. This is a paraphrased account, not medical advice.

1. Starting with patients’ experiences: dreams and connection

Why listen directly to dying patients?

Fighting disease is an important medical task, but dying patients also have emotional lives, relationships, and wishes. Understanding their experience requires listening to them directly.

Dreams or visions of deceased loved ones

Some patients report vivid dreams or a sense that deceased loved ones are present. Familiar encounters and companionship can bring comfort and a continued feeling of being loved. These experiences do not establish certainty about an afterlife.

2. Regret and priorities: what patients care about

Do people really have regrets at the end?

Being able to name regrets does not make them the centre of one’s remaining life. Immediate relationships, companionship, and comfort may matter more.

What matters to hospice patients?

The episode presents this ranking:

Patient rank Priority
1 Avoid placing a burden on family
2 Share time with loved ones
3 Obtain relief from pain and other symptoms
4 Find peace in one’s relationship with God
5 Retain mental awareness
6 Be able to die at home
7 Have one’s care wishes respected
8 Feel that one’s life has had meaning
9 Extend life as much as possible
10 Put financial affairs in order
11 Settle interpersonal conflicts

Individual priorities differ. The fourth item also includes inner peace beyond religious belief.

Medical students placed a meaningful life second, versus eighth for patients, and resolving conflicts seventh, versus eleventh. Their expectations did not always match patients’ priorities.

Are we pursuing the wrong things?

Belonging and interdependency are central to this discussion. Vulnerability makes the need for other people more visible; it does not create that need from nothing. Relationships are already part of how a person understands who they are.

A promotion, a purchase, or another achievement may bring satisfaction without providing lasting belonging. Beyond those accomplishments, someone is also a child, sibling, friend, or partner. Those identities do not disappear when work and other activities become impossible.

The host describes imagining his last day and thinking about the people around him, whether they were secure, and how his children would remember him—not the scale of his career. The contrast is with ordinary schedules: people say relationships matter, yet often allocate most of their time elsewhere.

Pain, comfort, and care at home

Fear of dying often includes fear of pain, uncertainty, and losing control. Without a clear conversation about what may happen and what support is available, patients and families are left to fill that uncertainty with frightening expectations.

Care therefore involves more than managing symptoms. It also involves explaining the situation and making space for the patient’s concerns and wishes. Honest information can preserve the ability to choose how to spend time rather than simply taking hope away.

Comfort may include familiar surroundings, loved ones, an acceptable level of awareness, and having care wishes respected. Hospitals provide necessary care, and home is neither possible nor preferable for everyone. The important distinction is between asking only what else can be done and asking what the person actually wants.

Facing extreme regret

Not everyone reaches the end at peace. Some patients are troubled by past wrongdoing, fear judgment, or urgently want forgiveness. Approaching death does not erase a personal history.

One patient in the interview was distressed by nightmares connected with his past. He later asked to see his daughter, held her for an extended time, expressed his love, and apologized. For her, it was an encounter unlike their earlier relationship.

The story is not a promise that all harm will be forgiven. It shows how distress can bring an avoided relationship into focus and lead to a late expression of responsibility and affection. That can matter without making intense regret the defining experience of every dying person.

3. More time, or better time?

Do dying people want more time?

Wanting more time is different from wanting time extended at any cost. Whether those days allow comfort and companionship, or are largely occupied by suffering and intervention, changes what “more” means.

The question is therefore not only how long a person can live, but how they can live. From a healthy position, that distinction can seem abstract. During illness, its demands and its effects on loved ones become part of each day.

Lifespan describes the length of life; healthspan concerns time lived in good health. The conversation does not make these opposing goals. It asks that life extension be considered alongside the experience of the time gained, rather than treating duration as an isolated number.

Is the longevity movement missing the point?

The pursuit of longevity can suggest that the right practices and measurements will make aging and death fully controllable. Disease and circumstances do not simply disappear, however, and confidence in particular interventions can exceed their support.

The discussion redirects attention from individual techniques to a whole way of living: movement, everyday food, social connection, and opportunities to slow down. These are aspects of present life, not only investments in extra years.

Examples from long-lived communities contrast daily activity and social routines with elaborate optimization programs. They are not presented here as a guaranteed recipe. The underlying question is what kind of life an effort to live longer is meant to preserve.

What contributes to a peaceful death?

The question begins with accomplishment: are people who have achieved more better able to leave peacefully? The response instead centres on love given and received.

Some of the people described lived outwardly ordinary lives but felt deeply fulfilled through their relationships. Their sense of connection did not depend on first demonstrating a large public legacy.

This concerns having loved, having been loved, and still being able to feel those bonds. It is not a guarantee of peace, nor a claim that someone who suffers failed to love enough. It illustrates that fulfillment in relationships need not follow the same scale as external measures of success.

What happens when someone dies alone?

Loneliness can involve more than an empty bedside. It can arise from years without dependable affection or from relationships in which closeness repeatedly brought harm.

One woman described in the interview had difficulty trusting that another person could genuinely love her. She had left a man who wanted to marry her because accepting that closeness felt unsafe. Her isolation was not a simple failure to reach out.

Near the end, she reported a dream in which he reassured her of his love. It brought some comfort without turning her earlier life into a tidy story.

The distinction is between having people nearby and being able to experience a relationship as trustworthy. An account of loneliness should not become blame directed at the lonely person.

When old wounds surface

A lasting injury can become a judgment about oneself: whether one is useful, lovable, or adequate in an important role. Such concerns may return during dying and sometimes receive a different response in a dream or inner experience.

The interview describes a man who lost an arm in childhood and worried about independence and his ability to provide. In his experience, former coworkers affirmed his work. A mother distressed about her child’s imprisonment described her parents expressing love and reassurance.

These accounts concern not only events but the identities built around them. Feeling accepted can offer relief. They remain individual experiences, however—not evidence that everyone’s trauma will resolve by itself at life’s end.

Should we worry about regrets?

A direct question about conflicts or regrets will often produce examples. A question about what matters most now may produce very different answers. Being able to name a feeling is not the same as making it the first priority.

Resolving conflicts coming last in the table does not establish that patients have no unresolved concerns. It does challenge the assumption that reviewing an entire life is necessarily their most urgent task. Familiar surroundings and present companionship may matter more.

Repeated emphasis on deathbed regret can make life sound like an examination in which every mistake must be avoided. The distinction here is between having made unsatisfactory choices and having nothing left except remorse about them.

4. Grief, changing hope, and continuing to live

Coping with a loved one’s death

No single action can remove the grief of losing someone. But having an opportunity to understand what is happening and express what matters can influence both the goodbye and its later memory.

Honest communication is one part. Denying an approaching death can compress the time available for arranging affairs, seeing people, and expressing wishes. Presence and shared support are another: families need room to talk, remember, and acknowledge fear.

One patient reported dreaming of making a familiar sauce with his deceased grandmother. He and his partner then prepared it together. The memory she retained included a shared activity and a familiar relationship, not only physical deterioration.

The way a goodbye unfolds can become part of bereavement, without determining one single correct course for grieving.

Losing his father

Kerr lost his father at twelve. For a child, that can mean losing not only a person but also the expected shape of family life and the sense that the world will continue as anticipated.

For years, he struggled to speak about his father or their last encounter. Later, work with dying patients and families brought him into contact with what had once been difficult to discuss. This does not romanticize loss; it describes how it changed his sensitivity to other people’s suffering.

Grief need not be understood as a stage completed once and for all. Its intensity changes, while shared experiences, habits, and ways of seeing the world can remain part of the bereaved person’s life.

How hope changes

Hope may initially mean recovery: treatment works, the body improves, and ordinary life returns. When that outcome becomes impossible, it does not necessarily remove everything worth anticipating.

Attention can turn toward comfort today, time with family, and then the future of loved ones. Someone may hope that children will be able to continue living well or that a partner will have support.

The interview also describes patients willing to be interviewed and filmed for the benefit of future people. They may never see the eventual results, yet still value contributing something.

Hope changes not only in size but in its object—from personal recovery, to the use of remaining time, to lives that will continue beyond one’s own.

Do people stop living once they know they are dying?

Awareness of limited time does not bring emotional life to an early end. A person may still care about a visit or conversation, reconsider a long-held concern, or experience a relationship differently.

The interview describes patients continuing to adapt and connect as physical abilities decline. Such changes need not be dramatic and do not require a final reconciliation. Responding to the present is itself part of living.

Seeing a patient only as someone waiting to die can obscure what they want today. Being unable to work or take part in former activities does not mean there are no wishes left to take seriously.

Do we all die alone?

Nobody can undergo another person’s death for them. That does not mean the preceding time must be disconnected from every relationship.

Some patients report companionship in dreams or inner experiences. What observers see—a person with closed eyes or less speech—may not capture everything the patient describes experiencing.

Dying can also rearrange relationships in the external world. Family members living in different places may pause their routines and gather, reorganizing time around a shared goodbye.

None of this removes real loneliness or makes practical companionship unnecessary. The distinction is between death being personal and a person necessarily having no connection at all.

Reconciliation and the language of fighting

A goodbye sometimes changes the weight of a disagreement. Relatives gather, former disputes become less central, and some patients find comfort in revisiting their own past. These changes are possible, not inevitable.

Describing dying only as a battle leaves a vocabulary of victory, perseverance, and defeat. A patient who focuses on comfort or companionship may then appear to be trying less, when the desired destination is actually a person, a familiar relationship, or relief from a burden.

Acknowledging that life is ending does not mean care ends. Care can continue with attention to what the person wants now: conversation, quiet, presence, or a particular unfinished task.

5. Illness, attention, and meaning

What his own cancer changed

Cancer and recovery abruptly slowed Kerr’s formerly restless life. Activities that had once been routine could no longer be carried out at the same pace.

His concerns also involved people who depended on him: how his illness would affect their lives and what would happen to them. Facing mortality brought responsibilities and attachments into focus alongside fear for himself.

During rehabilitation he gradually walked farther around his farm. One day, willow trees planted years earlier struck him as extraordinarily beautiful. The trees had not suddenly changed; his attention had.

The episode makes his discussion of living well personal rather than solely observational. Value was not only in doing something new, but in becoming able to notice what had already been there.

What is the meaning of life?

The response does not turn meaning into a single grand objective for everyone. It returns to love, shared experience, and mutual reliance: supporting others and being able to receive support.

A cherished version of oneself may be the dependable friend, caring parent, or partner who remained present in difficulty, rather than only the person receiving an award. Those relationships are part of a life, not decorations outside its supposed main purpose.

Meaning in this account therefore involves more than a future legacy. It includes whom one faces difficulty with, how one responds to others, and whether one can accept care as well as give it.

Children’s experiences

Children may not understand their situation through adult language about careers, regret, or a completed life, but they can still seek familiarity, safety, and companionship. The interview includes experiences involving people, animals, and reassuring places.

One girl described an encounter she found comforting while worrying that telling her mother might frighten her. She was making sense of her own feelings and trying to protect her mother’s at the same time.

The example extends the discussion of interdependency: vulnerability and concern for others can coexist. It is not a demand that every child be brave or accepting, but an indication that children also have an inner life worth hearing.
