What Matters at Life’s End: Christopher Kerr on Connection, Hope, and Care

It is easy to measure the present against a future milestone: another qualification, more money, one more completed project. Life will begin properly after that. But if the time available to us became much shorter, would those measures still be enough?

Christopher Kerr’s interview explores dreams, relationships, and hope near the end of life. The question I want to take from it is closer to home: Are the things receiving most of our attention also the things we would least want to lose?

Source: The Deathbed Doctor: Weeks Before Death, Patients Start Having Visions!, The Diary Of A CEO, published October 19, 2026; approximately 83 minutes. Cover image from the original video.

These notes draw on the public video description, chapters, and public interview transcript, with additional research. The later reflections are my own, not the guest’s words. This is not medical advice.

1. What the interview explores

Kerr is a hospice physician and the author of Death Is But a Dream. His conversation with Steven Bartlett addresses end-of-life dreams, the importance of connection, and how hope changes when recovery is no longer possible. It should not be read as scientific proof of an afterlife. Original interview

2. Regret: is it what matters most at the end?

Having an answer is not the same as having a priority

Around 18:18, the host brings up Bronnie Ware’s well-known accounts of end-of-life regrets. Kerr distinguishes answering a question about regret from making it a priority in everyday care. Interview transcript

The next section’s ranking helps frame that distinction. Having unfinished business and deciding what matters now are different questions. Still, a low ranking for resolving conflicts does not establish an absence of regret: the survey was not a direct measure of it.

This is not a claim that everyone finds peace

Around 33:36, Kerr acknowledges patients distressed by past wrongdoing. His observations should not become a claim that nobody dies with regrets. The discussion returns to regret around 51:35.

My own takeaway is not to make a regret-free life another compulsory achievement. We can take responsibility for harm, repair what can be repaired, and accept that some choices have no perfect answer.

For someone offering support, that means neither assuming a person needs to review every mistake nor insisting they let everything go. Listening to what they actually want to discuss matters more than arranging a tidy ending for them.

3. What matters to hospice patients? Eleven ranked priorities

Patients’ priorities are not always what others predict

Around 19:11, the interview introduces what matters to hospice patients, comparing their care goals with medical students’ predictions.

The corresponding study by Levy, Grant, Kerr, and colleagues, Hospice Patient Care Goals and Medical Students’ Perceptions: Evidence of a Generation Gap?, appeared online in 2020 and in a 2021 journal issue. It surveyed 80 hospice patients and 176 medical students using 11 care goals. Original research

The table paraphrases the patient priorities in the order presented in the episode. The full order is also reproduced in this research overview.

Patient rank Priority
1 Avoid placing a burden on family
2 Share time with loved ones
3 Obtain relief from pain and other symptoms
4 Find peace in one’s relationship with God
5 Retain mental awareness
6 Be able to die at home
7 Have one’s care wishes respected
8 Feel that one’s life has had meaning
9 Extend life as much as possible
10 Put financial affairs in order
11 Settle interpersonal conflicts

This is a group ordering of 11 specified goals in a particular sample—not every participant’s personal ranking or a universal ranking of life’s values. The fourth item refers to God in the questionnaire; around 27:36, Kerr discusses it more broadly as spiritual or existential peace. It does not establish that all patients are religious.

Three things worth noticing

First, avoiding a burden on family came first. For someone offering support, that suggests listening for concerns about the effect of illness on loved ones—not treating those concerns as evidence that the person actually is a burden.

Second, an observer’s imagined end-of-life concerns may differ from the patient’s. In the episode, medical students placed a meaningful life second, compared with eighth for patients; resolving conflicts was seventh versus eleventh. The study abstract reports statistically significant differences on seven of the eleven goals. Interview discussion · Research findings

Third, placing life extension ninth does not mean patients do not want to live, or that a treatment should be stopped. These are relative priorities among specified options. Lower-ranked goals can still matter, and individual preferences vary. The practical lesson is to ask the person rather than decide for them.

Relationships matter without making achievement meaningless

My takeaway is not that careers do not matter. Work can provide security, independence, and a way to care for others. The problem begins when it gets to make every decision.

“After this project,” “when I earn more,” and “once things settle down” can each be reasonable. Repeated for years, they can leave relationships with nothing but leftover time.

A question I find more useful is: if this year does not bring the position or outcome I want, what would I still want to have cared for well?

That does not require abandoning ambition. It means giving things that cannot wait a place alongside long-term goals. A promised visit can deserve the same seriousness as a work deadline.

4. Hope may need a different scale

Kerr discusses changing hope around 1:04:58. My reflection is that we often bind hope to one outcome: a recovery, a repaired relationship, a particular career path.

When that outcome becomes impossible, it can seem as though everything worth anticipating has disappeared with it. But losing a goal is not the same as losing every source of value.

Two questions can coexist: what can I still do toward the original goal, and how can the coming time be lived if the result does not change?

The first supports effort. The second keeps an entire life from depending on one result. Hope might become a conversation, a small shared plan, or something finally said clearly.

This is my interpretation, not a description of every patient. Hope can coexist with anger, fear, and disappointment. Nobody owes others a peaceful response to suffering.

5. Being present is not making someone “move on”

The discussion turns to bereavement around 53:20. It raises another question for me: does helping always mean making someone feel better immediately?

When someone is grieving, searching for the perfect reassurance can become a way of asking them to put their pain away. “Would you like to talk?” or an offer of specific practical help may leave more room for their actual needs. Silence need not mean failure.

Nor should this article become a demand for reconciliation. Some relationships are unsafe; some losses have no tidy resolution. Respect includes allowing a person not to explain, forgive, or have an answer yet.

6. Not another checklist for living correctly

Stories from the end of life can easily become one more source of pressure to optimize ourselves. I would rather keep a few open questions:

  • When did I last spend time with someone simply to be together?
  • Which important words keep getting postponed?
  • When I say I am busy, am I making a choice—or forgetting I can make one?

There is no need to rewrite a whole life plan. A concrete beginning might be contacting someone, arranging a long-delayed visit, or putting a phone away long enough to listen.

What I want to retain is not certainty about death, but attention to the present: do not wait until the future is fully arranged before making room for the people and life already here.
